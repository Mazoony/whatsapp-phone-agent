# Sudan Year 3 Secondary English — Teaching Reference

Use this reference only for students confirmed as Sudan Year 3 Secondary. The student's stated edition, teacher's notes, and assigned SPINE 6 or Treasure Island pages take precedence. These are original explanations and examples, not a reproduction of textbook or retelling passages.

## Course scope

The student is studying Sudan Practical Integrated National English (SPINE 6), *Treasure Island* written by Robert Louis Stevenson and retold by Stephen Colbourn, English tenses and their common time adverbs, passive voice, first/second/third conditionals, indirect speech, gerunds, infinitives after *make* and *let*, summary writing, composition, and letter writing.

Teach one point at a time: explain the rule briefly, model an original example, invite the student to try, then correct the specific error with a hint. Do not assume a particular SPINE 6 unit, test question, chapter sequence, or wording if the student has not provided it. For edition-specific questions, ask for the chapter/page or a short excerpt from the student's copy.

## Tenses and common time expressions

Time expressions are clues, not proof of tense. Choose tense from the meaning and time reference of the whole sentence. Keep the verb forms clear: base form, past form, and past participle are not interchangeable.

| Tense | Form | Main use | Common time expressions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present simple | subject + base verb (add -s/-es with he/she/it) | habits, facts, routines | always, usually, often, sometimes, every day, never |
| Present continuous | am/is/are + verb-ing | happening now or temporary activity | now, right now, at the moment, currently |
| Present perfect | have/has + past participle | past action connected to now; experience; unfinished time | already, just, yet, ever, never, since, for, so far |
| Present perfect continuous | have/has been + verb-ing | activity continuing from the past or recently continuing | since, for, all day, lately, recently |
| Past simple | subject + past form | completed event at a finished past time | yesterday, last week, ago, in 2020 |
| Past continuous | was/were + verb-ing | activity in progress at a past time; background action | while, as, at 8 pm yesterday |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | earlier of two past events | before, after, by the time, already |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + verb-ing | duration continuing up to a past point | for, since, until, before |
| Future simple | will + base verb | prediction, instant decision, promise | tomorrow, next week, soon, probably |
| Future continuous | will be + verb-ing | activity in progress at a future time | this time tomorrow, at 8 pm next week |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | completed before a future deadline | by, by the time, before + future point |
| Future perfect continuous | will have been + verb-ing | duration up to a future point | for + duration, by + future point |

Examples:
- She usually **studies** after dinner, but she **is studying** at the library now.
- We **have lived** here for five years. They **have been revising** since noon.
- I **finished** the exercise yesterday. While I **was reading**, my friend **called**.
- The train **had left** before we **reached** the station.
- I **will call** you tomorrow. At this time tomorrow, we **will be travelling**.
- By Friday, she **will have completed** the project; by June, she **will have been studying** English for three years.

Important distinctions:
- Use past simple with a finished past time (*yesterday*, *last year*, *two days ago*), not present perfect.
- Use *since* for a starting point (*since Monday*) and *for* for a duration (*for two hours*).
- Do not use continuous forms with many stative verbs when they describe a state (for example, *know, believe, own*).
- In a future time clause beginning with *when, before, after, until,* or *as soon as*, use a present form for a future meaning: *I will call when I arrive*, not *when I will arrive*.
- With *always/usually/often*, place the adverb before a main verb (*usually walks*) but after *be* (*is usually ready*). Place *yet* commonly at the end of questions and negatives; *already/just* commonly go before the past participle.

## Passive voice

Use the passive when the action or receiver matters more than the doer, or the doer is unknown. Form: the correct tense of **be + past participle**. The object of an active sentence becomes the passive subject. Add **by + agent** only when useful.

| Active tense | Passive pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Present simple | am/is/are + past participle |
| Present continuous | am/is/are being + past participle |
| Present perfect | has/have been + past participle |
| Past simple | was/were + past participle |
| Past continuous | was/were being + past participle |
| Past perfect | had been + past participle |
| Future simple | will be + past participle |
| Modal | modal + be + past participle |

Examples: *The committee reviews the applications* → *The applications are reviewed (by the committee).* *They were repairing the road* → *The road was being repaired.* *Someone has stolen my bicycle* → *My bicycle has been stolen.*

Keep the original tense and singular/plural agreement. Only verbs that take an object can normally form a passive. Do not add *by someone* when it gives no useful information.

## If conditionals

- **First conditional:** real or likely future result. **If + present simple, will + base verb.** Example: *If she revises tonight, she will feel more confident tomorrow.* Use a comma when the if-clause comes first. Do not normally put *will* in the if-clause.
- **Second conditional:** imagined or unlikely present/future situation. **If + past simple, would + base verb.** Example: *If I had more time, I would read another chapter.* *Were* is often used in formal hypothetical *If I were...* clauses.
- **Third conditional:** unreal past; the condition did not happen, so its past result is imagined. **If + past perfect, would have + past participle.** Example: *If they had left earlier, they would have caught the bus.*

For third conditional negatives, place *not* after the auxiliary: *If he had not forgotten the map, he would not have got lost.* Students may use contractions in ordinary writing, but full forms are often clearer in formal exercises.

## Indirect (reported) speech

When a past reporting verb introduces a report, adjust pronouns and time/place words to match the new speaker and situation. Often backshift the tense when the report is later:

- present simple → past simple: *“I need help,” she said* → *She said (that) she needed help.*
- present continuous → past continuous; present perfect → past perfect; past simple → past perfect.
- *will* → *would*; *can* → *could*; *may* → *might*.
- past perfect and *would/could/should/might* usually do not change.

Do not backshift a statement that remains generally true when the context supports keeping the present: *The teacher said that water boils at 100°C.*

Common changes include *now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, here → there,* and *this/these → that/those* when the viewpoint changes.

- Yes/no question: use **asked + if/whether + subject + verb**; remove question word order and do not add a question mark to the reported clause. *“Are you ready?” he asked* → *He asked if I was ready.*
- Wh-question: keep the wh-word, then use statement order. *“Where do you live?” she asked* → *She asked where I lived.*
- Command/request: **told/asked + object + (not) to + base verb**. *“Please wait,” he said* → *He asked me to wait.* *“Don't touch it,” she said* → *She told us not to touch it.*

## Gerunds and infinitives

A gerund is **verb-ing** used as a noun: *Reading improves vocabulary.* Use a gerund after a preposition: *interested in learning; good at writing; before leaving*. Common verbs followed by a gerund include *enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest,* and *keep*.

Use **to + base verb** after many verbs such as *want, hope, decide, plan,* and *agree*: *They decided to continue.* Some verbs can take either form with little change (*like, love, begin*); others change meaning (*stop doing* = end an activity; *stop to do* = pause in order to do something). Teach the pattern required by the actual sentence.

### *Make* and *let*

- Active **make + object + base verb without to**: *The coach made the players practise.*
- Active **let + object + base verb without to**: *Her parents let her travel.*
- Passive **be made + to-infinitive**: *The players were made to practise.*
- For permission in a formal passive, prefer **be allowed + to-infinitive**: *She was allowed to travel.* Avoid the incorrect pattern *was let to travel*.

## SPINE 6 and *Treasure Island*

Use SPINE 6 as the student's set textbook. Help with vocabulary in context, comprehension, grammar, writing tasks, and revision from the exact page or unit the student names. Do not invent unit titles, exercise answers, or official examination requirements; ask them to send the question or a short excerpt if it is not specified.

*Treasure Island* is Robert Louis Stevenson's adventure story; this course uses the retelling by Stephen Colbourn. The broad story follows Jim Hawkins and a voyage connected to a treasure map, where trust and danger arise among the crew. Common discussion points include courage, loyalty, greed, responsibility, deception, and growing up. Characters commonly associated with the story include Jim Hawkins, Long John Silver, Dr Livesey, Squire Trelawney, Captain Smollett, Billy Bones, and Ben Gunn. Use the student's assigned retelling for exact plot sequence, characterization, chapter details, and wording: different adaptations may shorten or change details. Do not reproduce passages or provide a chapter-by-chapter substitute for the book. Instead, explain themes and vocabulary in original wording, ask what happens in the student's edition, and analyze a brief passage they provide.

## Summary writing

Read the task and obey its word limit. Identify the central points, discard repetition and minor examples, and write them in a logical order using your own words. Keep the original meaning and tense; use concise linked sentences, correct grammar, and no personal opinion unless requested. Do not copy whole sentences from a passage. Check that every sentence contributes a main idea.

## Composition writing

First identify the topic, audience, purpose, and required length. Make a quick plan: opening, two or three ordered main points, and a closing. Use a clear paragraph for each main idea, suitable linking words, varied but accurate sentence structures, and relevant examples. Leave time to check verb tense consistency, subject-verb agreement, spelling, punctuation, paragraphing, and whether the composition answers the prompt.

## Letter writing

Use the format requested in the task; do not guess exam-specific conventions. An informal letter can use a friendly greeting, an opening that responds to the reader, organized body paragraphs, a suitable closing, and the writer's name. A formal letter should state its purpose clearly, use a respectful tone and organized paragraphs, include only relevant details, and close appropriately (for example, *Yours faithfully* when no recipient name is given, or *Yours sincerely* when the recipient is named, where that convention is expected). Follow the exact prompt if it specifies a different layout or closing.

## Teaching and feedback

Prefer a short explanation plus one original model sentence. Ask one focused practice question at a time unless the student requests a full exercise. When correcting writing, identify the issue, explain the rule briefly, and invite a corrected attempt; avoid rewriting a whole assignment in a way that prevents learning. Be encouraging and age-appropriate. If a question depends on a particular SPINE 6 unit, Treasure Island retelling chapter, or teacher's marking scheme not present here, say what is uncertain and ask for the page/question rather than inventing a textbook-specific answer.
